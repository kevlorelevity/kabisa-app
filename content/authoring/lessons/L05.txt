@lesson market-mama-mboga
title: Mama Mboga's Kibanda
level: 5
index: 1
theme: market
category: food-drink
culture: Every Nairobi estate has a mama mboga — the "vegetable lady" with a wooden stall (kibanda) by the road, selling tomatoes, onions and sukuma wiki in small bundles. Prices are quoted per heap ("tatu kwa mia" = three for a hundred) rather than per kilo, and almost everyone pays with M-Pesa. Nouns like nyanya (tomato) are n/n: the same word for one or many, so "this tomato" is nyanya hii and "these tomatoes" is nyanya hizi.
start: It's evening. You stop at the mama mboga's stall on your way home from work.
grammar: negative-present, na-have, demonstratives, numbers-money
gl: Nataka = "I want" — short for ninataka; this is how most Kenyans say it. #present-na
gl: Hizi = "these" — n/n plural (nyanya hizi = these tomatoes). #demonstratives
gl: Tatu kwa mia moja = "three for a hundred" — prices are quoted per heap.
gl: za hamsini = "(ones) for fifty" — za agrees with the n/n plural, the all-purpose choice in Kenyan speech. || Sanifu: vitunguu is ki/vi class, so "vya hamsini"
gl: fresh = "fresh" — English loanword used all the time at the market.
gl: shambani = "from / at the farm" — shamba + -ni (place suffix). #locative-ni
gl: till namba = "till number" — the M-Pesa "Buy Goods" number the seller displays.

> Mama Mboga: Karibu, mteja! Unataka nini leo? | Welcome, customer! What do you want today?
< Mteja: Habari, mama! Nataka nyanya na vitunguu. | Hello, mama! I want tomatoes and onions.
  x: Habari, mama! Unataka nyanya na vitunguu. ; Sina nyanya na vitunguu.
> Mama Mboga: Nzuri. Nyanya hizi ni tamu sana. | Good. These tomatoes are really sweet.
< Mteja: Nyanya hizi ni bei gani? | How much are these tomatoes?
  x: Nyanya hii ni wapi? ; Nyanya hizi ni nani?
> Mama Mboga: Tatu kwa mia moja. | Three for a hundred.
< Mteja: Sawa, nipe sita. | Okay, give me six.
  x: Sawa, nipe sitaki. ; Sawa, sina sita.
> Mama Mboga: Na vitunguu? Hizi ni za mia moja. | And the onions? These are a hundred.
  s: Na vitunguu? Hivi ni vya mia moja.
< Mteja: Sitaki nyingi. Nipe za hamsini tu. | I don't want many. Just give me fifty bob's worth.
  s: Sitaki vingi. Nipe vya hamsini tu.
  x: Nataka nyingi. Nipe za hamsini tu. ; Hutaki nyingi. Nipe za hamsini tu.
> Mama Mboga: Haya. Unataka sukuma pia? Ni fresh, inatoka shambani leo. | Alright. Do you want sukuma too? It's fresh, it comes from the farm today.
< Mteja: Hapana, asante. Leo sitaki sukuma. | No, thanks. Today I don't want sukuma.
  x: Ndiyo, asante. Leo sitaki sukuma. ; Hapana, asante. Leo hataki sukuma.
> Mama Mboga: Sawa. Ni mia mbili na hamsini. | Okay. It's two hundred and fifty.
< Mteja: Sina pesa mkononi. Una M-Pesa? | I don't have cash. Do you have M-Pesa?
  x: Nina pesa mkononi. Una M-Pesa? ; Huna pesa mkononi. Una M-Pesa?
> Mama Mboga: Ndiyo, till namba ni hii hapa. | Yes, the till number is right here.
< Mteja: Poa. Asante sana, mama! | Cool. Thanks a lot, mama!
  x: Pole. Asante sana, mama! ; Poa. Karibu sana, mama!
> Mama Mboga: Asante. Karibu tena! | Thank you. Come again!

@lesson family-photos
title: Who's in the Photo? Talking Family
level: 5
index: 2
theme: people
category: people
culture: Kenyans ask about family early and warmly — "Una familia?" isn't nosy, it's friendly. Family words are flexible: dada and kaka (sister, brother) are also how you politely address any young woman or man, and cousins are often just called brothers and sisters. People (m/wa nouns) take their own agreement: mtoto mmoja (one child), watoto wawili (two children), mke wangu (my wife) — but everyday family words like mama, dada, nyanya take yangu: mama yangu, dada yangu.
start: Lunch break at the office. Your colleague Achieng spots the photo on your phone's lock screen.
grammar: na-have, possessives, demonstratives, negative-present
gl: mrembo = "beautiful (person)" — m/wa agreement. Plural: warembo. #adjective-agreement
gl: Grace = Grace (name)
gl: David = David (name)
gl: Sarah = Sarah (name)
gl: Kampala = Kampala — capital of Uganda
gl: Nawakumbuka = "I miss them" — lit. "I remember them": na- (I, present) + -wa- (them) + -kumbuka (remember). #object-infixes
gl: Unawakumbuka = "Do you miss them?" — lit. "do you remember them?": u- + -na- + -wa- (them) + -kumbuka. #object-infixes
gl: wacheshi = "funny (people)" — m/wa plural of mcheshi. #adjective-agreement
gl: wee = "wow!" — surprise. #discourse-particles

< John: Hapana, hawaishi Nairobi. Wako Kampala na nyanya yao. | No, they don't live in Nairobi. They're in Kampala with their grandmother.
  x: Hapana, hawaishi Nairobi. Tuko Kampala na nyanya yao. ; Ndiyo, wanaishi Nairobi. Wako Kampala na nyanya yao.

  x: Ndiyo, sana. Na wewe, nina watoto? ; Hapana, sana. Na wewe, una watoto?

> Achieng: Ndiyo! Na wote ni wacheshi sana. | Yes! And they're all very funny.

@lesson directions-stage
title: Where's the Stage? Asking the Way
level: 5
index: 3
theme: directions
category: directions
culture: Kenyans give directions by landmarks, not street names: the big supermarket, the petrol station, "the tree near the church". The askari (security guard) at any building gate is the best person to ask — they know every shop and matatu stage nearby. "Si mbali" (it's not far) can mean anything from two minutes to twenty, so ask "Ni dakika ngapi?" (how many minutes?).
start: You're on foot in Kilimani and need the matatu stage to town. You ask the askari at a gate.
grammar: ko-location, negative-present, demonstratives, question-words
gl: Stage = "stage" — the matatu stop (Kenyans also write "steji").
gl: supermarket = supermarket (loanword)
gl: kuenda = "to go" — Kenyan form of kwenda. #infinitive-ku
gl: Ya kuenda tao = "The one for going to town?" — ya agrees with the n/n stage. #possessives
gl: kwa kona = "at the corner" — Kenyan; Sanifu prefers kwenye kona. #locative-ni
gl: pale pale = "right there" — doubling makes it exact.
gl: Enda = "Go!" — Kenyan imperative of kuenda. || Sanifu: Nenda! #imperatives
gl: hata kidogo = "(not) at all" — lit. "even a little"
gl: pole pole = "slowly"
gl: vizuri = "well / nicely"
gl: Kiswahili yako = "your Swahili" — Kenyan n/n agreement. || Sanifu: Kiswahili chako (ki/vi class) #possessives
gl: ya kwanza = "the first" (n/n agreement)

@lesson matatu-to-town
title: Matatu to Town
level: 5
index: 4
theme: transport
category: transport
culture: Matatus are Nairobi's privately run minibuses — loud music, graffiti art, and a makanga (conductor) hanging out of the door shouting the route. Fares aren't fixed: they rise at rush hour and when it rains, so it's normal to ask "Nauli ni ngapi?" before you get in. You get off by shouting where you want to stop. Imperatives are the plain verb: Panda! (get in), Shuka! (get off), Simama! (stop).
start: You're at the stage in Kilimani. A makanga is shouting for passengers to town.
grammar: future-ta, imperatives, numbers-money
gl: Kencom = Kencom — the big matatu stop in the CBD
gl: Tao = "Town!" — the makanga's call: this matatu goes to the CBD.
gl: kiti ya mbele = "the front seat" — Kenyan agreement. || Sanifu: kiti cha mbele (kiti is ki/vi) #sanifu-noun-classes
gl: iko na mtu = "is taken" — lit. "is with someone". Kenyan -ko na for "have". || Sanifu: kina mtu #na-have
gl: kawaida = "normally / usually"
gl: Nashuka = "I'm getting off" — short for ninashuka. #present-na
gl: Haya = "Alright!"
gl: benki = bank
gl: rush hour = rush hour (English)
gl: inapanda = "is going up" — kupanda = to climb / go up #present-na

@lesson saturday-plans
title: Plans for Saturday (and Swahili Time)
level: 5
index: 6
theme: friends
category: time
culture: Swahili time starts at sunrise, not midnight. 7 a.m. is saa moja ("the first hour"), 8 a.m. is saa mbili, noon is saa sita, and 6 p.m. is saa kumi na mbili. The trick: add or subtract six from the clock time. Kenyans switch between Swahili time and English time ("seven") all day — when it matters, check which one someone means! A day trip to Lake Naivasha (90 km away) is a classic weekend plan.

grammar: future-ta, time-swahili, helper-verbs, locative-ni
