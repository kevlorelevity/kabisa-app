@lesson directions-village
title: Which Way to the Hospital? Directions Upcountry
level: 3
index: 1
theme: directions
category: directions
culture: Outside the big cities, directions are given by landmarks — the shop, the wooden bridge, the primary school, the small hill — and almost never by street names. Kenyans describe a route in the present tense, step by step: "unaenda moja kwa moja, halafu unageuka kushoto" (you go straight, then you turn left). Locals are generous with directions; a greeting first ("Habari yako, kaka?") makes all the difference.

level: 3
index: 2
theme: weather
category: weather
culture: Kenya sits on the equator, so there's no winter or summer — there are rains. The long rains (masika) fall from March to May, the short rains (vuli) from October to December, and the dry season (kiangazi) comes in between. Nairobi is high up, so July is surprisingly cold and grey; Kenyans in jackets and scarves will tell you "ni baridi sana!". Weather is the safest small talk there is.

@lesson daily-routine
title: A Day in My Life: Swahili Time
level: 3
index: 3
theme: routine
category: time
culture: Swahili time starts at sunrise, not midnight: 7 a.m. is "saa moja asubuhi" (the first hour of the morning), and 6 a.m. is "saa kumi na mbili" (the twelfth hour). The trick is to add or subtract six. Kenyans also talk about "Kenyan time" — a meeting at "saa tatu" may start closer to ten. Days of the week are counted from Saturday: Jumamosi (Sat), Jumapili (Sun), Jumatatu (Mon), Jumanne (Tue), Jumatano (Wed), Alhamisi (Thu), Ijumaa (Fri).
start: Tea break at the office. Your colleague Achieng wants to know how you manage to be first in every morning.
grammar: time-swahili, present-na, question-words

gl: ninakunywa = "I drink" — -nywa = drink

@lesson feeling-unwell
title: Feeling Unwell: Head, Stomach and a Fever
level: 3
index: 4
theme: health
category: health
culture: "Pole!" (sorry) is the first thing any Kenyan says when you're unwell — and they'll say it again and again. Body parts are a great example of singular and plural pairs: mkono / mikono (arm / arms), mguu / miguu (leg / legs), jicho / macho (eye / eyes). In everyday Kenyan speech "-inauma" (it hurts) works for every body part: kichwa inauma, tumbo inauma. Clinics are everywhere, and "kliniki" is the word people use.
