# Level 2 — home, food & family. Level-1 grammar only; vocabulary focus (verbs, nouns, adjectives, singular/plural). Infinitives (ku-) appear as the dictionary form of verbs.

@lesson home-chores
title: Saturday Chores: Kazi za Nyumbani
level: 2
index: 1
theme: home
category: home
culture: Saturday morning is "kazi za nyumbani" time in most Kenyan homes: sweeping, mopping ("kupiga deki" — from the English "deck"), washing clothes by hand in a basin, and hanging them in the sun. Kenyans use different verbs for different kinds of washing: kuosha for dishes, cars and hands, kufua only for clothes. You share a flat in Kilimani with your friend Otieno.
start: Saturday, 9 a.m. Otieno bangs on your door with a broom in his hand.
grammar: present-na, infinitive-ku, adjective-agreement, possessives
gl: Otieno = Otieno (a Luo name)
gl: kazi za nyumbani = "housework, chores" — lit. "work of the home"
gl: Jumamosi = Saturday
gl: vyombo = dishes || Sanifu: chombo / vyombo (ki/vi class)
gl: kupiga deki = "to mop" — deki from English "deck"
gl: kabati = cupboard
gl: ndoo = bucket
gl: hita = (water) heater
gl: mbovu = broken / rotten
gl: hata = even
gl: Chumba yangu = "my room" — Kenyan agreement. || Sanifu: chumba changu (ki/vi class)
gl: chafu = dirty
gl: kitanda = bed || Sanifu: kitanda / vitanda (ki/vi class)
gl: taka taka = rubbish / garbage — Kenyans double it || Sanifu: taka
gl: chakula cha mchana = lunch (lit. "food of midday")
gl: rahisi = easy / cheap
gl: tamu = sweet / tasty
gl: nyingi = many / a lot (n/n plural)
gl: peke yangu = "on my own, alone"
gl: Tuko pamoja = "we're together" — we're in this together

> Otieno: Niaje, John! Leo ni Jumamosi — siku ya kazi za nyumbani! | Hey, John! Today is Saturday — chores day!
< John: Poa, Otieno. Mimi ninaosha vyombo jikoni. | Okay, Otieno. I'm washing the dishes in the kitchen.
  x: Poa, Otieno. Mimi ninafua vyombo jikoni. ; Poa, Otieno. Wewe ninaosha vyombo jikoni.
> Otieno: Sawa. Mimi ninafagia sakafu. | Okay. I'm sweeping the floor.
< John: Poa. Sabuni iko wapi? | Cool. Where's the soap?
  x: Poa. Sabuni iko nani? ; Poa. Sabuni ni ngapi?
> Otieno: Iko kwa kabati, karibu na ndoo. | It's in the cupboard, next to the bucket.
  s: Iko kabatini, karibu na ndoo.
< John: Asante. Maji ni moto au baridi? | Thanks. Is the water hot or cold?
  x: Asante. Maji ni wapi au baridi? ; Asante. Sabuni ni moto au baridi?
> Otieno: Maji ni baridi. Stima iko, lakini hita ni mbovu. | The water is cold. There's power, but the heater is broken.
< John: Sawa, hata maji baridi ni sawa. | Okay, even cold water is fine.
  x: Sawa, hata maji baridi ni chafu. ; Sawa, hata maji moto ni baridi.
> Otieno: Na nguo? Nani anafua nguo leo? | And the clothes? Who's washing the clothes today?
< John: Mimi ninafua nguo zangu, na wewe unafua zako. | I'm washing my clothes, and you're washing yours.
  x: Mimi ninapika nguo zangu, na wewe unapika zako. ; Mimi unafua nguo zangu, na wewe ninafua zako.
> Otieno: Haha, poa! Na chumba yako? Ni safi? | Ha, fine! And your room? Is it clean?
  s: Haha, poa! Na chumba chako? Ni kisafi?
< John: Hapana, chumba yangu ni chafu sana! Ninatandika kitanda sasa. | No, my room is really dirty! I'm making the bed now.
  s: Hapana, chumba changu ni kichafu sana! Ninatandika kitanda sasa.
  x: Hapana, chumba yangu ni safi sana! Ninatandika kitanda sasa. ; Hapana, chumba yangu ni chafu sana! Ninakula kitanda sasa.
> Otieno: Sawa. Mimi ninatoa taka taka, halafu ninapika chakula cha mchana. | Okay. I'm taking out the rubbish, then I'm cooking lunch.
< John: Safi! Unapika nini? | Great! What are you cooking?
  x: Safi! Ninapika nini? ; Safi! Unapika wapi?
> Otieno: Ugali na sukuma. Ni rahisi na tamu! | Ugali and sukuma. It's easy and tasty!
< John: Poa sana. Kazi za nyumbani ni nyingi, lakini tuko pamoja! | Very cool. There's a lot of housework, but we're in it together!
  x: Poa sana. Kazi za nyumbani ni nyingi, lakini niko pamoja! ; Poa sana. Kazi za nyumbani ni tamu, lakini tuko pamoja!

@lesson kitchen-ugali
title: In the Kitchen: Ugali and Sukuma
level: 2
index: 2
theme: kitchen
category: food-drink
culture: Ugali — stiff maize porridge eaten with your right hand — is Kenya's national dish, and sukuma wiki (collard greens, lit. "push the week") is its faithful partner because it's cheap enough to stretch the money to the end of the week. Making ugali has its own verb: kusonga, to stir and press the flour into the boiling water with a wooden mwiko. Kenyans say "ninasikia njaa" — lit. "I hear hunger" — for "I'm hungry".
start: Your neighbour Mama Njeri has invited you into her kitchen to learn the most important Kenyan recipe.
grammar: infinitive-ku, present-na, adjective-agreement
gl: Mama Njeri = "Njeri's mum" — your neighbour
gl: jikoni = "in the kitchen" — jiko (kitchen / stove) + -ni. #locative-ni
gl: Tunaanza aje? = "How do we start?" — aje = how (Kenyan). || Sanifu: Tunaanzaje?
gl: Kwanza = "first"
gl: sufuria = cooking pot (sufuria)
gl: jiko = stove / cooker
gl: Vikombe tatu = "three cups" — Kenyan. || Sanifu: vikombe vitatu (ki/vi class)
gl: inachemka = "is boiling" — -chemka = boil. || Sanifu: maji yanachemka
gl: unga ya mahindi = "maize flour" || Sanifu: unga wa mahindi
gl: kidogo kidogo = "little by little"
gl: unasonga = "you stir (the ugali)" — kusonga is the special verb for making ugali
gl: mwiko = wooden cooking stick
gl: kwa nguvu = "hard, with strength"
gl: ngumu = hard / difficult
gl: Mkono yangu = "my arm / hand" — Kenyan. || Sanifu: mkono wangu (m/mi class)
gl: inauma = "it hurts" — Kenyan. || Sanifu: unauma / unaniuma
gl: tayari = ready
gl: Macho yangu = "my eyes"
gl: inawasha = "it stings / itches" — Kenyan. || Sanifu: yanawasha
gl: kawaida = normal
gl: unakaanga = "you fry" — -kaanga = fry
gl: mafuta = (cooking) oil
gl: Inanukia = "it smells good" — -nukia = give off a (nice) smell
gl: Ninasikia njaa = "I'm hungry" — lit. "I hear hunger"
gl: Chumvi = salt
gl: Unaonaje = "What do you think?" — lit. "how do you see (it)?"
gl: kwa somo = "for the lesson"
gl: mpishi = cook / chef

> Mama Njeri: Karibu jikoni, John! Leo unajifunza kupika ugali. | Welcome to the kitchen, John! Today you're learning to cook ugali.
< John: Asante, mama! Ninapenda ugali sana. Tunaanza aje? | Thanks, mama! I love ugali. How do we start?
  x: Asante, mama! Ninapenda ugali sana. Tunamaliza aje? ; Asante, mama! Unapenda ugali sana. Tunaanza aje?
> Mama Njeri: Kwanza, unaweka maji kwa sufuria, halafu unawasha jiko. | First, you put water in the pot, then you light the stove.
  s: Kwanza, unaweka maji kwenye sufuria, halafu unawasha jiko.
< John: Sawa. Ninaweka maji ngapi? | Okay. How much water do I put in?
  x: Sawa. Ninakula maji ngapi? ; Sawa. Ninaweka maji wapi?
> Mama Njeri: Vikombe tatu. Maji inachemka, halafu unaweka unga kidogo kidogo. | Three cups. The water boils, then you add the flour little by little.
  s: Vikombe vitatu. Maji yanachemka, halafu unaweka unga kidogo kidogo.
< John: Ni unga ya mahindi? | Is it maize flour?
  s: Ni unga wa mahindi?
  x: Ni maji ya mahindi? ; Ni unga ya wapi?
> Mama Njeri: Ndiyo, unga ya mahindi. Sasa unasonga — unakoroga na mwiko kwa nguvu! | Yes, maize flour. Now you "songa" — you stir hard with the wooden stick!
< John: Wee, ni kazi ngumu! Mkono yangu inauma. | Wow, it's hard work! My arm hurts.
  s: Wee, ni kazi ngumu! Mkono wangu unauma.
  x: Wee, ni kazi rahisi! Mkono yangu inauma. ; Wee, ni kazi ngumu! Mkono yangu inanukia.
> Mama Njeri: Haha! Pole. Ugali iko tayari. Na sukuma — unakata vitunguu na nyanya. | Ha! Sorry. The ugali is ready. And for the sukuma — you chop onions and tomatoes.
< John: Ninakata vitunguu sasa. Macho yangu inawasha! | I'm chopping the onions now. My eyes are stinging!
  s: Ninakata vitunguu sasa. Macho yangu yanawasha!
  x: Ninakunywa vitunguu sasa. Macho yangu inawasha! ; Anakata vitunguu sasa. Macho yangu inawasha!
> Mama Njeri: Ni kawaida! Sasa unakaanga vitunguu kwa mafuta, halafu nyanya na sukuma. | That's normal! Now you fry the onions in oil, then the tomatoes and sukuma.
< John: Inanukia vizuri sana! Ninasikia njaa. | It smells really good! I'm hungry.
  x: Inanukia vizuri sana! Ninasikia baridi. ; Inanukia vizuri sana! Unasikia njaa.
> Mama Njeri: Chumvi kidogo… na chakula iko tayari! Unaonaje? | A little salt… and the food is ready! What do you think?
< John: Ni tamu sana, mama! Asante sana kwa somo. | It's delicious, mama! Thank you so much for the lesson.
  x: Ni chafu sana, mama! Asante sana kwa somo. ; Ni tamu sana, mama! Pole sana kwa somo.
> Mama Njeri: Karibu! Sasa wewe ni mpishi! | You're welcome! Now you're a cook!

@lesson family-visit
title: Sunday Lunch with Wanjiru's Family
level: 2
index: 3
theme: people
category: people
culture: Being invited to someone's family home — "kwetu" (our place), often outside the city — is a real honour in Kenya. You greet every person in the room, starting with the elders, and the mother of the house will make sure you eat far more than you planned. Kenyans describe people freely and kindly: tall, short, big, young. Describing words for people come in pairs: mrefu (one tall person), warefu (tall people).
start: Sunday afternoon in Kiambu. Your friend Wanjiru has brought you home to meet her family.
grammar: possessives, adjective-agreement, ko-location, present-na
gl: Wanjiru = Wanjiru (a Kikuyu name)
gl: nyumbani = "at home" — nyumba + -ni. #locative-ni
gl: yote = "all, whole" — familia yangu yote = my whole family
gl: Mama Wanjiru = "Wanjiru's mum"
gl: Baba Wanjiru = "Wanjiru's dad"
gl: Ninafurahi = "I'm happy" — -furahi = be happy
gl: kuwa hapa = "to be here"
gl: nje = outside
gl: kwa shamba = "on the farm" || Sanifu: shambani
gl: Kamau = Kamau (a Kikuyu name)
gl: kama wewe = "like you"
gl: Watu wa familia yetu = "the people in our family"
gl: wanacheza = "they're playing" — -cheza = play
gl: mbwa = dog
gl: ya furaha = "happy" — lit. "of happiness"
gl: mezani = "at the table" — meza + -ni. #locative-ni
gl: inanukia = "smells good"

@lesson clothes-colours
title: Shirts, Shoes and Colours at Toi Market
level: 2
index: 4
theme: market
category: commerce
culture: Toi Market and Gikomba are where Nairobi buys its clothes — much of it "mitumba", second-hand clothing sold from huge bales. Sellers call out to every passer-by, the first price is always high, and "bei ya mwisho" (last price) is part of the game, not the end of it. Colour words for things are mostly n/n: shati nyeupe (white shirt), viatu nyeusi (black shoes). Bluu, kijani and njano never change.
start: Saturday at Toi Market. A seller waves a shirt at you from his stall.
grammar: adjective-agreement, numbers-money, present-na, ko-location
gl: Toi = Toi Market, a big clothes market in Nairobi
gl: Unatafuta = "you're looking for" — -tafuta = look for
gl: Ninatafuta = "I'm looking for" — -tafuta = look for

gl: suruali = trousers
gl: viatu = shoes || Sanifu: kiatu / viatu (ki/vi class)
gl: soksi = socks
gl: rangi = colour
gl: bluu = blue
gl: nyeupe = white (n/n)
gl: kijani = green
gl: nyeusi = black (n/n)
gl: mpya kabisa = "brand new"
gl: saizi = size
gl: ya kati = "medium" (lit. "of the middle")
